A state of twenty-two thousand square kilometres

The wettest state
governed by clans, not itself.

Meghalaya joined India in 1972 built on a foundation the state has never fully owned: clans, syiems and nokmas hold most of the land, three Sixth Schedule councils share power with the Assembly in Shillong, and a non-tribal Indian citizen generally cannot buy an acre. This is a working map of that arrangement: its coal, its Christianity, and the rain that never quite gets captured.

Baseline

A plateau surrounded by plains, owned by no single authority

Elevation runs from 150 metres near the Bangladesh plains to 1,961 metres at Shillong Peak. Rainfall runs from the wettest place on Earth to chronic dry-season scarcity a few kilometres away. Almost none of the land in between belongs to the State.

The Supreme Court's 2019 ruling kept the rat-hole method banned and ordered illegally mined coal auctioned through Coal India, but enforcement runs up against thousands of small, clan-held seams no authority has ever had the right to survey. The December 2018 Lumthari (Ksan) mine flooding, in which a group of miners were trapped underground, remains the disaster Meghalaya's coal debate keeps returning to.

Own tax revenue has grown from ₹1,450 crore in 2017-18 to ₹4,720 crore budgeted for 2026-27 — but the State still can't tax land it doesn't administer. The Meghalaya Transfer of Land (Regulation) Act and the Sixth Schedule keep most land, and most of its value, off any register the State controls.

Budgeted deficit vs the audited outturn

The MFRBM Act targets a fiscal deficit of 3–3.5% of GSDP. The audited outturn keeps landing far above it.

Even a record ₹7,439 crore in capital outlay is a quarter of what the State spends in a year. The widely cited "₹10,211 crore" capital figure folds in ₹2,731 crore of debt repayment, which builds nothing.

In short

A state that governs alongside its clans, not over them

The Sixth Schedule and a century of land law kept most of Meghalaya's hills out of any market the State itself controls, and that arrangement protected matrilineal, tribal land rights that elsewhere in India simply vanished. But the same architecture means the State can't unilaterally build a railway, license a mine, or run a power line without a clan's consent — and when consent isn't given, the result is sometimes a missing rail line, sometimes a flooded coal shaft. The open questions — the Inner Line Permit, the reservation formula, the Assam border, dependable power in winter — are still being negotiated village by village, exactly as they always have been.
